Lasers

Self-Consistent Multiphysics Dynamics for PINN-Driven Telecom Laser EDA

Project Vision

Lasers develops a compact physics-informed model for telecom laser Electronic Design Automation (EDA). The model is designed for Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), linking engineering inputs, semiconductor carrier transport, band-energy dynamics, photon density, electromagnetic frequency, and heat generation to laser performance outputs.

The aim is not to reproduce every microscopic quantum process. The aim is to build an interpretable drift-diffusion simulator that can be calibrated with experimental data and operated inside an EDA workflow for telecom laser design.

Modelling Principle

The core modelling principle relies on the classic drift-diffusion model for carriers, coupled with frequency-domain wave optics and lattice heat equations. This structure integrates Poisson's equation for electrostatics with carrier continuity equations for electron and hole densities, driving transport via drift and diffusion fields.

This gives the PINN a structured physics backbone while leaving uncertain physical parameters (such as recombination rates and gain coefficients) available for calibration. It is intended as a practical middle path between a fully microscopic quantum simulator, which is too expensive to run, and a pure neural network model, which is limited by data sparsity.

Corrected Formula Set

The revised version 2.2 organizes the semiconductor laser model into a consistent formula set:

  • Electrostatics: Poisson's equation defines the quasi-static electric field that drives carrier transport.
  • Carrier conservation: Electron and hole densities follow drift-diffusion continuity equations with volumetric generation and recombination sources.
  • Band-energy dynamics: Effective transition energy is transported and diffused, perturbed by electrical work, and relaxed toward a temperature-dependent equilibrium band energy.
  • Photon density: Photon population is generated by radiative recombination, reduced by absorption, and limited by photon lifetime or escape time.
  • Heat conservation: Temperature responds to electrical work, non-radiative recombination, photon absorption, photon decay, and cooling through package or heat-sink effects.
  • Electromagnetic output: The frequency-domain wave equation is linked to the band-energy equation through photon frequency and angular frequency.

Band-Energy Closure

The effective transition band energy is the key bridge between semiconductor transport and optical output. In this model, band energy is not allowed to fluctuate freely. It relaxes toward a local temperature-dependent equilibrium value, making the model more physically interpretable and numerically stable.

A simple first-stage closure uses a linear temperature relation. A Varshni-type empirical relation can be used when measured material data are available. Higher temperature lowers the effective band gap, shifting the output frequency downward and wavelength upward.

fγ = ηγe / h

Ωγ = ηγe / ℏ

λγ = ch / (nrηγe)

PINN Training Formulation

The model is prepared for experimental-data-assisted PINN operation. The input vector includes current, voltage, heat-sink temperature, cavity dimensions, reflectivities, and material parameters. The target vector contains optical power, output frequency, output wavelength, frequency variation, maximum temperature, and electrical-to-optical efficiency.

The loss combines data agreement, physics residuals, boundary constraints, energy consistency, and stability penalties. Physics residuals are formed from the electrostatic, carrier, current velocity, band-energy, photon, thermal, and electromagnetic equations.

L = Ldata + λphysLphys + λbcLbc + λenergyLenergy + λstableLstable

EDA Workflow

  1. Define device geometry, material parameters, electrical inputs, optical cavity properties, and heat-sink conditions.
  2. Solve or train the PINN-calibrated drift-diffusion semiconductor model.
  3. Predict telecom laser output metrics: Popt, fout, λout, Δf, Tmax, and ηEO.
  4. Apply a design objective that balances target optical power, target frequency, frequency stability, thermal limits, and efficiency.
  5. Search for the optimized laser design variables inside the EDA loop.

The intended workflow is:

engineering inputs → PINN-calibrated drift-diffusion simulator → telecom laser output metrics → optimized design

Task 1.2 Coupled Multiphysics Solver

The second simulator task integrates the discrete physical models into a fully coupled 2D Electro-Optic-Thermal solver. It solves for quasi-static potential and drifting carriers (using a Finite Volume Method in CarriersSolver), 2D transverse electromagnetic modes (using a vector Helmholtz form in EMSolver) which analytically incorporates facet mirror losses and exports the PhotonPopulation, and lattice heat transport (in ThermoSolver).

The solver accounts for mutual feedback loops: temperature-dependent stimulated gain degradation, photon population driving carriers through stimulated recombination, Auger/SRH non-radiative recombination heating, and quantum defect heating.

A key milestone is numerical stability. By calibrating the carrier equation Jacobian scaling to 1.0e-26, the nonlinear trust-region dogleg solver (CoDoSol) converges cleanly (ierr = 0) in every steady-state iteration. Additionally, using conservative Zero Normal Flux boundary conditions for symmetry margins prevents non-physical carrier depletion (negative densities) at boundary corners, yielding smooth, physically meaningful results.

Task 1.4 Electro-Optical Efficiency Spectrum

To calibrate the resonance behavior of the edge-emitting cavity, we developed a frequency sweep tool centered around the design resonance frequency f0 = 193.4 THz (extending across a 1% width range of 192.43 THz to 194.37 THz). The tool couples the 2D transverse mode profile with a 3D single-mode Lorentzian cavity resonance factor.

The resulting spectrum resolves a clean single-mode cavity resonance peak with a linewidth of 30.61 GHz. The Peak Wall-Plug Efficiency (WPE) reaches 5.398% at resonance and decays down to 0.0013% at the sweep boundaries, demonstrating the high spectral selectivity of the cavity.

Task 1.5 2D Parameter Sweeps & Operating Options Impact

To understand the trade-offs between design parameters and device efficiency, we performed 2D sweeps of Wall-Plug Efficiency (WPE) against frequency and three critical operating options: mirror reflectivity (R), cavity length (L), and lattice temperature (T0).

The sweeps reveal that the peak efficiency occurs at an optimal reflectivity of approximately R = 0.3 (representing cleaved facets) due to the trade-off between mirror loss and internal photon density. Longer cavities increase the round-trip gain but also accumulate internal absorption loss. Thermal heating above 300 K severely degrades efficiency due to carrier leakage and Auger recombination heating.

Task 1.6 Quasi-3D (2.5D) Simulator & Spatial Hole Burning

To capture longitudinal spatial hole burning (SHB) and asymmetric facet coating effects without the prohibitive computational cost of a 3D FEM mesh, we implemented a Quasi-3D (2.5D) Simulator. It couples the 2D Elmer solver output parameters (active area Aact = 9.6 × 10-9 cm2, confinement factor Γ = 0.05, active region injection current I'active ≈ 4.265 A/cm) with a 1D longitudinal optical propagation and carrier rate solver.

A stable log-carrier Newton-Raphson solver (x = ln(N)) solves the steady-state rate equations at each longitudinal slice z. Below is the spatial profile comparison between symmetric cleaved facets (R1 = R2 = 0.3) and asymmetric HR/AR coated facets (R1 = 0.9, R2 = 0.05):

For the symmetric cavity, the profiles are perfectly symmetric along the cavity axis. For the asymmetric cavity, the internal optical power peaks strongly near the front facet (z = L), which depletes carriers locally and causes a visible asymmetric dip in carrier density N(z) (longitudinal spatial hole burning).

Task 1.7 Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) & Stand-alone Design Application

To deliver real-time optimization capability for diode laser design without the computational overhead of solving coupled differential equations iteratively, we developed a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) surrogate model and packaged it into a stand-alone design application.

Physics-Informed Machine Learning Framework

We generated a dataset of 1,500 converged multidimensional sweeps using our calibrated 2.5D solver. A PyTorch-based Deep Neural Network is trained to map five design parameters (facet reflectivities R1 and R2, cavity length L, temperature T0, and active injection current Iactive) directly to the 105 output metrics (including global optical power, WPE, terminal current, and 51-point spatial profiles for carrier density N(z) and optical power P(z)).

The network utilizes a custom Physics-Informed Loss that scales and enforces the steady-state carrier continuity rate equations across the cavity axis. This forces the model to respect the physical limits of carrier recombination and stimulated emission, yielding an exceptionally high fidelity surrogate (R2 > 0.99) with instantaneous (5 ms) evaluation speed.

Stand-alone Design Application (Streamlit)

Using the trained model, we developed a premium interactive Streamlit-based dashboard (run_pinn_app.py). The dashboard provides:

  • Real-Time Parametric Sweeps: Instantaneous recalculation of output power, wall-plug efficiency, and terminal current as the user adjusts design sliders.
  • Profile Visualizations: Real-time charts of carrier density and longitudinal optical field, allowing visual capture of spatial hole burning dynamics under asymmetric facet coatings.
  • Dynamic Design Guidance: Dynamic physical analysis text explaining the state of the laser (e.g., thermal droop warning, near-threshold behavior, or reflectivity skew insights).
